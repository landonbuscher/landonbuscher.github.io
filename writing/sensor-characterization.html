What a stationary gyroscope actually reports

2026-08-05 · LSM6DSOX, Allan variance

A stationary gyro never reads zero. Characterization is how you learn how wrong it is, and how fast that gets worse.

I don't think we stop to appreciate the electronic quasi-magic happening all around us enough. Before starting my journey in embedded programming, to say I took the inner workings of computers for granted would be an understatement. Chief among these quasi-magical activities is the ability to turn ones and zeroes into a physical number that can be used in a control system to represent some real-world quantity.

The fact that there is a little mass measured in micrograms sitting inside a MEMS gyroscope, and that it can somehow measure angular rate, is a truly mind-boggling feat to try to wrap your head around. But despite this little mass' best efforts, its raw output is rather unreliable, and it is the role of embedded software engineers to characterize and calibrate these sensors to make their output less erroneous.

Sensor characterization

Sensor characterization is the process of identifying the deviation between the expected output of a sensor and its raw output, and attributing parts of that deviation to different sources of error. In a gyroscope the premise is simple: if the part is completely stationary, you would expect every axis to report zero motion for the entire lifespan of the program. Alas, due to the economics of engineering, a perfectly calibrated gyroscope is a lost cause, and several factors contribute to spurious output.

Characterization is not an optional step, and especially not for gyroscopes — skipped, reported values can wander as far as 30 degrees from truth in minutes.

Sources of error

In characterizing a gyroscope, we are looking to identify:

  • Offset bias
  • Bias repeatability
  • Angular random walk
  • Rate random walk
  • Bias instability

Offset bias

This is the simplest source of error to correct for. If your gyroscope is perfectly stationary, the mean of each axis' output should be zero. If it isn't when the gyro first starts, you can take the mean of the raw output and subtract it from all future outputs. 

corrected_output = raw_output - constant_bias

Bias repeatability

Offset bias is not perfectly constant — it changes slightly during each run of a program, and it also changes from run to run. That run-to-run variation is bias repeatability: a measure of how much the offset bias changes each time you power on the gyroscope. To correct for it, the offset must be measured and subtracted at every startup during a calibration phase, with the gyroscope held perfectly stationary.

Angular random walk

The raw output of a gyroscope — or any sensor, for that matter — is never completely steady. White noise causes the reading to jitter up and down rapidly, and that movement is mostly random. But when you integrate angular rate to find an angle, the sum of that white noise causes the angle to wander over time. Your angle reading performs a random walk away from the true value. An Allan variance analysis, explained below, quantifies this noise and its impact.

Rate random walk

Bias repeatability measures run-to-run variation; rate random walk measures the in-run variation — how the constant bias drifts over the life of a single program. The offset, like the integrated angle, performs a random walk, driven primarily by environmental and complicated mechanical processes. Because of this, confidence in a startup-calibrated reading should decrease over time. Like angular random walk, it does not gravitate toward any particular value.

The difference is the underlying noise. Angular random walk comes from white noise — rapidly changing, clearly visible sample-to-sample. Rate random walk comes from a much slower noise called Brownian noise. Rate random walk itself cannot be corrected for, since it is caused by unpredictable mechanical factors, though you can mitigate it with filters.

Bias instability

Angular random walk is helped by averaging: take the mean of increasingly large groups of samples and compare adjacent groups, and the difference between them shrinks as white noise gets averaged away. Rate random walk is hurt by averaging: keep growing the groups and the difference starts to grow again as environmental and mechanical drift shows up in your averages.

In between the two is a region where averaging over larger samples neither helps nor hurts — the noise between adjacent groups is identical. That is the period of bias instability, where flicker noise lives: a type of noise sitting between white and Brownian that can never be completely averaged away. It will make more sense after the Allan variance.

The Allan variance

Imagine a six-hour sample of raw, perfectly stationary gyroscope data collected at 100 Hz — one sample every 10 milliseconds. We want to identify two things: how the readings change in the short term, and how they change in the long term.

Start by splitting the data into groups of 50 ms and averaging their values. The data is quite volatile here — with only five readings per group, white noise heavily affects each average, so comparing one group to its neighbors will likely show a large difference.

So add more data to the buckets by growing them to 10 s. White noise now has a much smaller effect on each average, and adjacent groups land much closer together as the white noise gets averaged out. Graphed, you would reason that as group size increases, the deviation between adjacent groups must decrease.

There comes a point, however, where making the groups larger no longer affects the deviation between adjacent pairs at all. The groups are now sized such that white noise is no longer visible. On the graph, this is flat: group size increases, deviation stays constant.

Take it one step further and make the groups very large — say 3000 s. Certainly there is no white noise left. But now the offset bias has begun to drift between groups. This is the period of rate random walk, and the graph turns back upward: as group size continues to increase, the deviation between adjacent groups begins to grow.

That is an Allan deviation plot, and it looks like this:

This is a graph I produced after recording raw data from my gyroscope overnight. On the left, the y-axis is labeled Allan deviation — the root mean square of the differences of all adjacent pairs, scaled by 1/√2. Put simply, it is a measure of how much, on average, adjacent pairs differ. Along the x-axis is tau, in seconds: the size of the buckets we kept adjusting. The shape is exactly what was described — a downward slope as bucket size increases, a flat period (or, in this case, a minimum), and a rebound where deviation starts to climb again.

The downward slope on the left is the period of angular random walk: the values of tau where white noise dominates the integrated angle. Since white noise changes rapidly, averaging it out with larger tau is very effective.

The upward slope on the right is the period of rate random walk. Rate random walk is primarily a measure of environmental and mechanical change, and that is not a quick process. So as tau gets sufficiently large, we actually see adjacent groups start to drift apart again — each group is now so large that they have different biases.

The minimum is the period of bias instability, sitting between the two. Tau is big enough that you can no longer see angular random walk — though, importantly, it is still there — but not so large that rate random walk has appeared. It is the exact point where the marginal benefit of averaging equals the marginal cost, to think about it economically.

Where the noise actually comes from

I found bias instability the hardest of the bunch to understand, so it is worth walking through the underlying noise theory. Angular random walk, rate random walk, and bias instability are all the result of noise in the raw gyroscope output, but each comes from a different type of noise.

White noise is the easy one: it moves up and down rapidly and becomes visible with smaller averages — this is the noise responsible for angular random walk. Brownian noise moves up and down very slowly and becomes visible with larger averages — this is the noise responsible for rate random walk. In the middle is flicker noise, which is responsible for bias instability.

All three are present at every frequency; they only become visible at certain average sizes. White noise gets averaged away when you take longer averages. Brownian noise stays hidden if you take shorter averages, though it is still there. And since flicker noise sits in the middle, you will always have some that is too fast to average away and some too slow to have shown up yet. That is why bias instability is a flat line — no matter how you adjust the average size, you remove exactly as much noise as you reveal.

You will notice bias repeatability never came up in the Allan deviation analysis. By definition it cannot: it is the variation in bias between runs, and the plot only ever depicts a single run.

What the constants are for

From this plot we can read off N (angular random walk) at tau = 1, K (rate random walk) at tau = 3, and B (bias instability) at the minimum, divided by a constant of 0.664.1 These are the constants you plug into a filter to quantify how much noise is present in the gyroscope.

These should be read off fitted lines, not the raw curve. The raw curve at a single tau carries the estimation noise of that one bucket size; the fit uses the whole slope.

With them you can finally tell a filter how much to trust your gyroscope. N describes how noisy any single reading is, K describes how quickly the startup calibration goes stale, and B tells you the accuracy floor of the part. They let a Kalman filter weigh the gyro against other sensors instead of guessing.

Controlling this little vibrating mass starts with knowing exactly how much to believe it — and that is what sensor characterization is for.

1. The 0.664 comes from the relationship between the flicker noise floor and the Allan deviation minimum. ↩
